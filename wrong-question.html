What If Hopkins Was the Wrong Question?

Every other page on this site documents a decision that went badly. This one does the opposite: it takes the sharpest crayon in the box — explore alternatives — and shows what an hour of using it would have produced. The answer turns out to touch Hopkins, the Marina, the ferry, and where Berkeley puts homeless services.

A worked example of the framework, not a finding against anyone · August 2026

The Hopkins debate was framed as a contest over a fixed resource. Should the street space belong to parked cars, or to bicycles?

Once those became the only two choices, the argument became zero-sum. Every gain for one side had to come out of the other. The reasoning on both sides grew more detailed and more heated, and all of it rested on one assumption nobody examined:

The pie was fixed. It doesn’t have to be.

This site’s crayon box counts seven ways Berkeley could answer a fiscal or service problem, and finds that four are worn to stubs while three keep their points. One of the sharp ones is explore alternatives. This page picks it up. The corridor itself is examined separately as Case Study A in the framework.

Fisher and Ury, in Getting to Yes, give this failure a name and a remedy. The failure is bargaining over positions — the specific outcome each side demands. The remedy is to step back to interests, the underlying needs those positions were meant to serve, and then look for options that satisfy the interests on both sides at once. That is what a win-win actually is: not a compromise in which each side gives up part of its position, but a different option that serves what each side was really after.

Hopkins is a clean example, because the positions and the interests come apart so plainly.

⚠ The positions
  • Keep the parking
  • Build the separated cycle track

Mutually exclusive. One must lose.

◆ The interests underneath
  • Get the shopping home without carrying it far
  • Reach the neighborhood businesses
  • Let a senior get to the pharmacy
  • Be safe on the street
  • Meet the city’s climate commitments

Not in conflict. Nobody wants the other side to fail at these.

Stated as positions, the two sides cannot both win. Stated as interests, almost nothing separates them — the cyclist wants the pharmacy reachable too, and the driver is not against the climate targets. The fight was real, but it was a fight about the instrument, conducted as if it were a fight about the goal.

It is worth being exact about the instrument, because the public argument has not been. The contested item was not a bike lane in the ordinary sense — a painted lane alongside traffic is uncontroversial and largely already there. It was a Class IV separated bikeway, a cycle track physically divided from the roadway by posts, curbs, planters or a row of parked cars. That is a different object with different consequences: it consumes street width permanently, it changes how a fire engine reaches a curb, and it complicates refuse collection, loading and passenger drop-off in ways paint does not.

Those consequences are precisely what the Fire Chief was being asked about on 28 July 2026, and precisely what the record put before Council said had been reviewed when it had not — see the Hopkins Street fire review. Collapsing “cycle track” into “bike lane” is how a specific engineering dispute became a referendum on whether you support cycling.

It also matters for the argument this page is making. Fewer car trips reduce the pressure that makes a separated track feel necessary in the first place. A street with meaningfully less traffic is a street where paint and speed management may be sufficient — which is the cheaper intervention, the one that does not fight the fire code, and the one nobody was arguing for because the debate had already narrowed to two fixed designs.

There is also a question about the safety evidence that we cannot answer. The case for separated tracks is usually made on cyclist injury rates against motor vehicles, which is a real and well-studied benefit. Whether anyone has modelled that alongside the rest of the ledger — delayed emergency response on a narrowed street, refuse and delivery vehicles obstructing a lane they can no longer pull clear of, conflicts between cyclists and pedestrians at crossings and bus stops, and the behaviour of cyclists themselves — we do not know. Such an analysis may well exist. We have not seen one cited in the Hopkins record, and we have not searched the literature for it.

We raise it as a question rather than a finding, because a question is what it is. If the integrated analysis exists, it belongs in the staff report. If it does not, that is worth knowing before narrowing a street.

And here the missing option is almost comic. Berkeley does not merely have a research university on its doorstep. It has the Institute of Transportation Studies, and within it SafeTREC — the Safe Transportation Research and Education Center, founded in 2000, spanning public health, city and regional planning, public policy and transportation engineering, running a Center for Pedestrian and Bicyclist Safety whose stated goal is eliminating pedestrian and cyclist fatalities and serious injuries. It is about a mile from Hopkins Street.

A city arguing for years about the safety consequences of a street design has, within walking distance, one of the country’s leading centers for exactly that question. Neither the Institute nor SafeTREC is mentioned once in nineteen months of Council transcripts, in the agenda record, or in the technical basis the July 2026 item relied on — which was a Fire Department review, not an academic analysis. Berkeley researchers do get cited in Council debate on other technical questions; wildfire and vegetation management is one. Just not this one.

That is the crayon box again, in its purest form: the option was not rejected, it was not considered.

There is a second thing the positions hide. Each of them only works for part of the population, and the argument has largely been over whose part matters more.

Illustrative, not measured — no mode-share data for these trips has been published, and the bars show relative reach rather than exact shares. The fourth band is the one most often left out of this argument and is probably the largest: Berkeley is built on a hill, and the difference between “can ride a bicycle” and “can ride a bicycle up Marin Avenue with a week’s shopping in February” is most of the population. Cycling provision serves the trip down. Nothing currently serves the trip back. The distinction that matters most, though, is the third band. Some people drive because it is easiest; some drive because nothing else works — a disability, an injury that will heal in six weeks, a parent managing a toddler and a week’s shopping. For that group there is no substitute, and any proposal that treats their spaces as available is not a serious proposal. Conflating the two groups is what makes this argument so bitter: each side hears the other denying a need rather than debating a convenience.

Set out this way, the connector’s claim is narrower than it first sounds, and better for it. It does not serve everyone. It takes pressure off the first two bands — which is precisely what would leave more spaces for the third. The people who must drive do better under a proposal that reduces competition for parking than under one that rations it, and better still than under one that removes it.

This is also why the bicycle argument keeps running aground on weather and on the hill. A lane is an excellent answer in September on the flat and a poor one in a February downpour on a grade, and a policy that assumes year-round uphill cycling is asking people to be someone they are not. The asymmetry is the giveaway: the ride down to the shops is easy and the ride back is the whole problem, which is why the bicycle answer works best for exactly the trip that needs it least.

A connector does not replace cycling provision. It makes cycling usable as a choice rather than a commitment — ride down when it is dry, take the shuttle back up with the shopping, take it both ways when it rains. That hybrid is what most people would actually do, and it is unavailable today.

Once the interests are on the table, the question changes shape. Not “who gets the curb on Hopkins?” but:

How can Berkeley make neighborhood errands easier without requiring every resident to drive?

That question has more than two answers. Street reconfiguration is still one of them. So are parking management, delivery partnerships, demand-responsive service — and a connector built for errands, which is the one this page follows, because it is the one nobody costed.

Hopkins was never fundamentally about parked cars, and it was never fundamentally about bicycles. It was about how people reach neighborhood businesses, how they carry groceries home, how a senior gets to a pharmacy, and how a family makes four stops on a Saturday without treating each one as a separate driving trip. Those are mobility problems. Berkeley debated curb allocation.

The option that was never costed

Call it a Berkeley Community Connector: a small, frequent, fare-free shuttle optimized for one job that no existing service does well — short errand trips within the city, with cargo.

It would not replace AC Transit or duplicate BART. Transit networks are built for different jobs. BART moves people across a region. AC Transit is built for corridor throughput across a large service area. Neither is designed for the trip that ends with someone carrying two bags four blocks uphill, and neither should be. That job currently has no service designed for it, which is precisely why it keeps being addressed through street design instead.

Neither is a city department. Both are governed by a board that meets in public and buys operations on a contract. That is the shape of the thing, and it is not speculative.

There is a sharper way to put this. The Emery Go-Round already runs into Berkeley, and it already does the grocery run. Its Hollis route crosses the city line, turns off Hollis and makes a loop up 9th Street specifically to reach Berkeley Bowl West, which is the route’s northern terminus. The stop is named for the store. Service runs every fifteen minutes from just after six in the morning, and it is fare-free and open to anyone — including any Berkeley resident standing at the stop.

So a neighboring city of about 13,000 people funds a shuttle that detours into Berkeley to bring its residents to a Berkeley grocery store, at a frequency no Berkeley service matches. Berkeley, with roughly ten times the population, has nothing comparable in either direction — not to Berkeley Bowl West, not to the Oregon Street store, not to Monterey Market, not to Solano or North Shattuck or Fourth Street.

The model is not hypothetical and it is not distant. It is operating on Berkeley streets, on a fifteen-minute headway, paid for by somebody else.

Now hold that next to Hopkins.

Monterey Market is the North Berkeley version of the same destination: a grocer people travel to rather than stumble across, drawing customers from well beyond walking distance, with parking that has been contested for as long as anyone can remember, sitting on the corridor the city spent years arguing about. It is, in every functional respect, the problem Emeryville solved by routing a shuttle to a grocery store.

Emeryville looked at that trip and built a route around it. Berkeley looked at the same trip and held a multi-year fight about who should get the curb. One of those cities treated “how do people get their groceries home” as a transportation question with transportation answers. The other treated it as a territorial dispute between drivers and cyclists, and then held a vote to see who won.

That is what the missing crayon costs. Not a shuttle — a whole category of answer.

What one option unlocks in four other places

The reason to take this seriously is not Hopkins. Hopkins merely exposed the gap. The same missing option appears in four other places on this site, each one already documented.

The ferry: the same fixed pie, on the waterfront

Hopkins was a fight over a curb. The waterfront is heading into a fight over a parking lot, and it has exactly the same shape.

The City’s own transportation plan projects peak weekday demand of 421 spaces against a baseline of 250 — 171 short before ferry service begins. The Draft Environmental Impact Report describes 425 to 475 spaces being used by the project’s new trips, on a site that holds about 404 today. Whatever the final numbers, the structure is set: ferry passengers and existing waterfront users — the people who come for Cesar Chavez Park, Adventure Playground, Shorebird Park, the sailing clubs, the launch ramps, the sunset — will be allocating a fixed supply between them.

Two constituencies, one lot, and the arithmetic guarantees that one of them loses. That is the Hopkins argument again, relocated three miles west and not yet begun.

There is a second problem, and it is more awkward. The business plan models 68% of ferry passengers arriving by car and 5% by public transit, and the EIR projects 1,127 new vehicle trips a day to the waterfront. The project’s stated objective is to reduce single-occupancy vehicle travel; its own model says it works by generating it. The environmental case rests on those drivers otherwise having driven 25 miles to San Francisco — a comparison nobody has tested, because the business plan contains no analysis of how many riders would be diverted from BART, AC Transit or the Richmond ferry rather than from cars.

This is the part that ought to trouble the people who argued hardest for Hopkins. The case for narrowing a street was that Berkeley should make driving less convenient in order to meet its climate commitments. The case for the ferry accepts a project whose access model is two-thirds cars and several hundred new parking spaces. Both positions can be held. Holding both without noticing is what happens when each decision is judged on its own.

A frequent connector does not resolve the ferry’s finances or its permits. It does change the parking arithmetic from a fixed pie into a variable one, and it is the only thing on the table that would make the project’s access consistent with the climate rationale used to justify the street it fought over. It appears nowhere in the project record.

The Marina’s isolation

The waterfront hotel is separated from downtown, the campus and BART by a freeway interchange. Visitors increasingly choose accommodation by asking whether they can stay somewhere without renting a car, and for the Berkeley Marina the honest answer today is no. That is a competitive disadvantage the City has never addressed with a service, in a fund the City Manager described in 2021 as “on the brink of insolvency.” A connector does not fix the Marina Fund. It removes one of the reasons the waterfront underperforms as a destination.

Four decisions on three quarters of a mile of University Avenue

Between 2023 and 2025 the City acquired or converted four properties along one stretch of University Avenue:

  • 1001–1011 University (with 1925 Ninth) — the former Premier Cru building, acquired for $6.7 million, intended largely for below-market-rate housing with part of it as an interim Council Chamber.
  • 1461 University — the Rodeway Inn, bought and converted to permanent supportive housing with more than $14 million in state Homekey funds, 2023.
  • 1512 University — the Howard Johnson, interim housing operated by Dorothy Day House, contract approved November 2024.
  • 1761 University — the Signature Inn, the CareBridge post-arrest diversion program, an $8 million grant accepted on consent in September 2025.

That is about three quarters of a mile, and at least $28.7 million of identifiable public money. Each decision was made on its own, in its own staff report, against its own funding deadline. Several are individually defensible.

The first of the four has already failed on its own terms. The building was bought largely for below-market-rate housing, with part of it to serve as an interim Council Chamber while Old City Hall sat seismically unsafe. Neither happened as intended: the Council still meets at the school district boardroom, and the building now holds a scatter of nominal-rent tenancies — the Berkeley Food Network at $12 a year in base rent, Meals on Wheels, some City uses, some vacant space.

When the reason for buying something stops being true, there is a decision to be made: keep it, sell it, transfer it, lease it at market rate, or commit it to a different public purpose on the record. Berkeley made none of those. It kept the temporary arrangement and moved on. That pattern has a name in the framework this site measures against: a failure to STOP — treating discontinuation as a real management option rather than an admission of defeat. The acquisition is set out at length as Case Study J.

What does not appear anywhere in the record is the cumulative question: what is Berkeley’s plan for University Avenue? It is the road most visitors take from the freeway to the university — the approach to the campus and the city’s commercial spine. Four decisions, four reports, no document that assesses them together or asks what the corridor is becoming.

This is what a small option set produces at scale. Siting turns on access — whether residents of a facility can reach services, and whether a neighborhood believes it is absorbing more than its share. A connector changes the arithmetic of both, because it decouples where a facility is from what its residents can reach. That does not make siting easy. It makes the option set larger than the map, which is the difference between a plan and a sequence of separately reasonable decisions.

Premier Cru is Case Study J in the framework. Between 2020 and 2025 Berkeley sited roughly eleven homeless facilities and programs; essentially all landed in the Flats and West Berkeley, none in the hills — see the Oxford Elementary site. Two of the University Avenue conversions are also the subject of a separate finding about consultation scheduled after the money was committed — see District 4 shelter siting.

Neighborhood commercial districts and seniors

Solano, North Shattuck, Fourth Street and Downtown all depend on customers who currently arrive by car, and all face the same constrained parking. Meanwhile the residents least able to drive — and least able to carry a load any distance — are the ones for whom every errand becomes a logistical problem. Both are access problems. Neither is a curb problem.

The hardest question, which is also the first one

Everyone asks about groceries, and they are right to. The reason people drive to a grocery store is the load they carry home, and a fixed-stop loop can reintroduce exactly the walk the trip was meant to avoid.

Which is why the Berkeley Bowl West stop is worth sitting with. The Emery Go-Round is a commuter shuttle built to connect Emeryville to MacArthur BART. Nothing about that job requires detouring across the city line and looping a residential block to reach a grocery store, and yet the route does exactly that, and the store is the northern terminus. Somebody, at some point, concluded the grocery trip was worth designing for.

That is not proof the load problem is solved — no origin-destination data for that stop appears to have been published, and how people actually carry their shopping home from it is unknown. It is precisely the kind of thing a serious option search would go and find out, and it is available for the cost of a phone call to the Emeryville Transportation Management Association.

It is also worth being precise about how large the objection really is. The load argument is usually made against the shuttle and rarely against the bicycle, and the two do not survive the comparison. Anyone who can cycle home with loaded panniers can almost certainly walk two blocks with two bags. The physical ask is comparable, and the bike version requires balancing it at speed in traffic.

So the group genuinely defeated by a short walk from a stop — someone with a mobility impairment, a large family shop, several children in tow — is substantially the same group already defeated by a bike lane. It is not an additional population the connector fails. It is the population that neither existing position ever served, and the one a demand-responsive return leg could actually reach.

This is a habit Berkeley is not good at. The City takes visible pride in going first — on a long list of policies — and that instinct has produced real things. It is a poor substitute for the less glamorous discipline of finding somebody who has already solved your problem and asking them how it went.

Berkeley is unusually well placed to notice this, because it has repeatedly been on the paying side of the arrangement. In 2014 Berkeley passed the nation’s first tax on sugar-sweetened beverage distributors, absorbing the industry campaign against it and the litigation risk that came with going first. Two years later Albany, Oakland and San Francisco passed their own versions — drafted with the benefit of Berkeley’s experience. Berkeley also spent roughly $12.4 million building the Dona Spring Animal Shelter, opened in 2013, and now shelters animals for Albany, Piedmont and Emeryville under service agreements.

Neither of those is a scandal, and being the city others learn from is a reasonable thing to want. But it is worth noticing that Berkeley is very good at being the one who pays to find out, and much less practiced at being the one who arrives second with the other city’s operating data in hand. Going first makes a campaign mailer. Going second, deliberately, is usually cheaper and works more often. Emeryville has a shuttle. Berkeley could ask how it is going.

That is a design constraint, not a fatal objection, and it is the thing a pilot exists to answer. Plausible responses include demand-responsive routing on the return leg, siting the small number of pick-up points for walkability rather than coverage, coordinating with stores on cart handling, or pairing the service with delivery for the heaviest trips. Which of those works is an empirical question that six months and a modest budget would settle.

What none of this requires is perfect substitution. If a meaningful share of errand trips shift out of single-occupant cars, parking pressure eases for the people who genuinely need to drive, commercial districts get more reachable, and the street-space argument gets less zero-sum — without anyone having to lose it.

Why this is a Category 2 problem

The framework this site measures against sorts municipal work into three categories. This is not Category 1 — Berkeley does not need to own a shuttle to benefit from one. It is a textbook Category 2: an undertaking whose scale, expertise and beneficiaries require genuine partners.

Category 2 carries a condition, and it is the part that usually gets skipped: “Collaboration must be binding, not aspirational. A Category 2 proposal should identify each partner contribution, governance right, continuing obligation, service area, benefit allocation, and exit condition.”

Listing willing partners is not a plan. Emeryville’s answer is a plan: a nonprofit association with a public board and defined seats, funded by an assessment the payers voted on, operating through a contract that can be re-bid. Berkeley’s version might look different — the plausible partners here are the commercial district associations, Visit Berkeley, AC Transit, BART, waterfront stakeholders, and the ferry project if it proceeds — but it needs the same skeleton.

One further condition, from the same framework: provider neutrality. The operating model should be chosen by comparing city staff, a transit agency, a nonprofit, a private contractor and a partnership on equal criteria. Emeryville landed on a nonprofit contracting to a private operator. That may or may not suit Berkeley. It should be the result of a competitive process, not an assumption written into the first staff report.

What a serious version would ask

A six-month pilot, competitively bid, with private, nonprofit and public operators all eligible, reporting against measures that can be compared directly with the infrastructure alternatives:

  • Cost per car trip removed from the corridor — the same measure applied to street reconfiguration, so the two can be ranked against each other.
  • Cost per resident given access they do not have today.
  • Whether corridor merchants and property owners would fund a benefit district, as Emeryville’s did.
  • A specific answer to the grocery-load problem above.

The cost of finding out is roughly what Berkeley has spent on individual consultant contracts approved this year without discussion.

What this page does not claim

That a shuttle would have resolved Hopkins. It might not have. The point is narrower and harder to dispute: it was never costed, so nobody knows.

That this is the only missing option. It is one. The argument is about the size of the option set, not about this particular answer to it.

That the option set was the only thing wrong with Hopkins. It was not. The item that passed rested on a claim that the Fire Department had reviewed the adopted design. The Fire Chief told the Council on the record, four hours before the vote, that it had not. The recommendation was never corrected and the plan was adopted anyway, and that is a separate and more serious failure — documented at the Hopkins Street fire review. A wider option set would not have excused it. This page is about a different fault in the same decision.

The point is not the shuttle

Every other analysis on this site documents something that went wrong. This one is here because a critique that only ever prosecutes is easy to dismiss, and because the framework is worth more as a tool than as a verdict.

Berkeley’s decisions are routinely framed as a choice between two positions, each with a constituency, each requiring the other to lose. That framing feels like rigor — the arguments get very detailed — but it is what a small option set looks like from the inside. The most valuable question in a public meeting is rarely which side should win. It is:

What problem are we actually trying to solve?

An hour of asking that on Hopkins would have cost nothing, and it would have produced at least one option that serves the ferry, the Marina, the commercial districts, the siting fights, and the people on both sides of the curb argument. Nobody asked.

Hopkins is decided. Most of what it touches is not. The waterfront parking fight has not started. University Avenue has no plan. The ferry’s use permit still needs findings from the Planning Commission, the Zoning Adjustments Board and the Council, and its construction money does not exist yet. Every one of those is a live decision where somebody could still ask what else is on the table.

The pie was fixed on Hopkins because nobody checked. It does not have to be fixed on the next one. That is not optimism about this Council — it is a standard the next decision can be measured against, and this page exists so that measurement is possible.
